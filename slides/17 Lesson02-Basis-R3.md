We have to get used to this so in $\mathbb{R}^3$ consider the basis

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbf{e}_1= (1,0,0),\quad \mathbf{e}_2 = (0,1,0),\quad \mathbf{e}_3= (0,0,1)\\
\mathbf{f}_1= (1,1,1), \quad \mathbf{f}_2 = (1,-1,1), \quad \mathbf{f}_3= (1,1,-1)
\end{aligned}
$$

First of all is the set $\mathbf{f}_i$ linearly independent?

You can show this easily and since there are three of them it is a basis of the three-dimensional vector space $\mathbb{R}^3$

If a vector $\mathbf{v}$ has coordinates $(c_1,c_2,c_3)$ in the $\mathbf{e}_i$ basis what are the coordinates in
the $\mathbf{f}_i$ basis?

So we have to express the initial basis vectors in the target basis

$$
\begin{aligned} (1,0,0)=a_{11} (1,1,1)+a_{21} (1,-1,1)+a_{31} (1,1,-1)\\ (0,1,0)=a_{12} (1,1,1)+a_{22} (1,-1,1)+a_{32} (1,1,-1)\\ (0,0,1)=a_{13} (1,1,1)+a_{23} (1,-1,1)+a_{33} (1,1,-1)
\end{aligned}
$$

These are **three** linear systems of **three** equations in **three** unknowns. Let us write them down explicitly
